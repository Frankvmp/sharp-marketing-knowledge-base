# Repeat buying and repertoires

## Core statement

Most buying is repeat behaviour. Buyers typically return to a limited personal repertoire of brands or stores, with habit, convenience, and familiar options reducing the effort needed to choose.[^repeat-buying]

## Definitions

- **Repeat buying** is buying the same category or brand twice in succession.
- **Repertoire** is the set of brands a buyer purchases within one category.
- **Behavioural loyalty** is a relatively stable restriction of purchases to a personal repertoire; it does not require exclusive purchasing or strong stated preference.[^repeat-buying]

## Mechanism and relationships

The number of possible products, providers, stores, and channels is far larger than buyers can or want to evaluate on every occasion. Familiarity, previous use, memory, and ease of purchase therefore concentrate behaviour on a limited set of options. High-consequence decisions can increase evaluation, but the observed pattern remains limited search and substantial repeat buying for many categories, including cars, banking, and insurance.[^repeat-buying]

Loyalty is widespread even where switching is easy and alternatives appear similar. It can form without meaningful product differences, and it can subsequently create attitudinal preference. This means behavioural loyalty should not automatically be explained as the reward for superior differentiation.[^natural-loyalty]

## Evidence and data

Car-brand purchases show repeat rates close to 50 per cent in the cited evidence, although buyers face roughly 60 brands and years can pass between purchases. Buyers considered about two brands on average, with the previous brand usually among them. A web-panel study of more than 10,000 households found average active-month visits of 1.2 book sites, 1.3 CD sites, and 1.8 travel sites.[^repeat-buying]

A television panel showed that households with access to 40 channels watched about 12 weekly; those with access to more than 101 watched about 20. The result illustrates repertoire behaviour despite low switching costs and many available alternatives.[^natural-loyalty]

## Conditions and limits

First category purchases, hobbies, and choices with large consequences can lead to more active search. They should not be treated as evidence that thorough comparison is the normal pattern. Loyalty programmes can disproportionately reward buyers who were already most loyal, so programme enrolment is not by itself evidence of incremental loyalty.[^natural-loyalty]

## Decision implications

Assess the current repertoire and the relevant buying situation before assuming a buyer will consider a brand. Improve the likelihood of being noticed, recalled, and easy to buy. Evaluate loyalty activity against incremental behaviour, not participation or rewards distributed to existing heavy buyers.
