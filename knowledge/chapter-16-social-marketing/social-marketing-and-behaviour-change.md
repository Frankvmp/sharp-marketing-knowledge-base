# Social marketing and behaviour change

## Core statement

Social marketing applies commercial-marketing principles and other disciplinary approaches to promote voluntary behaviours that benefit individuals and communities. Its objective is social good rather than direct commercial gain.[^definition]

## Definitions

The social-marketing product is usually a behaviour, attitude, or practice rather than a conventional branded good or service. Social marketing can operate at brand level or category level, and it may complement commercial marketing in public or private organisations.[^definition]

## Mechanism and relationships

Voluntary behaviour change requires people to perceive a meaningful exchange between the costs of changing and the benefits offered. Behaviour can be difficult to change when it is habitual, socially reinforced, pleasurable, private, or tied to addiction. Category-level work can change broader norms and availability, while brand-level work can connect a cause or behaviour to a particular organisation.[^definition]

## Evidence and data

The chapter defines social marketing around voluntary behavioural change and distinguishes it from commercial marketing by its aim and its behavioural product. It uses examples of health, environmental, and consumer-choice behaviours to illustrate the scope of application.[^definition]

## Conditions and limits

An organisation pursuing a social objective may still create ethical issues if it spends shareholder or customer resources on a cause without considering stakeholder interests. Social marketing should not use misleading claims or unsupported fear appeals.[^definition]

## Decision implications

An agent should identify the exact voluntary behaviour, intended social benefit, exchange offered to the audience, and level of intervention before calling an initiative social marketing.
