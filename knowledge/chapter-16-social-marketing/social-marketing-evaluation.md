# Social marketing evaluation

## Core statement

Social-marketing evaluation tests whether the intended behaviour changed and whether the initiative contributed to that change. Reliable evaluation requires pre-initiative benchmarks, clear behavioural measures, and comparison with what would likely have happened without the initiative.[^evaluation]

## Definitions

A benchmark records the current level of behaviour before an initiative. A control group is an unexposed comparison group measured over the same period. Social-desirability bias occurs when respondents report behaviour that appears more acceptable than their actual behaviour.[^evaluation]

## Mechanism and relationships

Evaluation begins by defining success, assumptions, the behaviour, and the measurement approach. Baseline behaviour is captured before activity, followed by post-initiative measurement and, where possible, comparison with a control group. Measures should reduce recall error and social-desirability bias by using natural time periods, logical frequency scales, and question designs that support accurate reporting.[^evaluation]

## Seven-step evaluation method

1. Define what success looks like and state the assumptions linking the initiative to the intended change.
2. Operationalise the target behaviour and identify the appropriate unit, place, and time for measurement.
3. Establish pre-initiative behavioural benchmarks and capture comparable post-initiative measures.
4. Use multiple measures or mixed methods where practical.
5. Reduce self-reporting error and social-desirability bias through evidence-based question design.
6. Capture explanatory variables that can help interpret success or failure.
7. Keep the evaluation independent of initiative implementation to reduce bias.[^evaluation]

## Evidence and data

The chapter provides a seven-step evaluation approach and emphasises that late evaluation can be uninformative when no baseline data exists. It also identifies the difficulty of measuring private or socially sensitive behaviours.[^evaluation]

## Conditions and limits

A measured change does not automatically establish causation. Other events, policy changes, seasonality, self-selection, and measurement error can create apparent change. Control groups and explicit assumptions strengthen but do not eliminate these limitations.[^evaluation]

## Decision implications

An agent should define the behavioural outcome and baseline before launch, preserve measurement assumptions, compare post-initiative results against a credible counterfactual, and distinguish reported attitudes from observed or reliably measured behaviour.
