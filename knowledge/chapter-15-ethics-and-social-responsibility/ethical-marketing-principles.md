# Ethical marketing principles

## Core statement

Marketing decisions have an ethical dimension when they affect people beyond the immediate commercial transaction. Ethical evaluation balances freedom of choice with responsibility to society, and it can require more than legal compliance.[^principles]

## Definitions

Ethics consists of socially learned and shared ideas about right and wrong. Law sets enforceable rules; ethical questions can remain when conduct is legal but potentially harmful, unfair, exploitative, or misleading.[^principles]

## Mechanism and relationships

Ethical issues arise when the interests of a company, an individual consumer, and society differ. A decision can be assessed by asking who benefits, who bears harm or risk, whether affected parties have meaningful choice, and whether the consequences are acceptable beyond the firm’s immediate objective.[^principles]

## Evidence and data

The chapter uses questions about unhealthy products, restrictions, individual choice, and government intervention to show why a purely legal answer may not resolve an ethical disagreement.[^principles]

## Conditions and limits

Freedom of choice does not settle questions involving vulnerable groups, material information imbalance, addiction, harmful products, or external effects borne by others. Social responsibility should likewise not be used to make unsupported claims of public benefit.[^principles]

## Decision implications

An agent should separate legality from ethical acceptability, identify affected groups, and state the freedom, harm, and responsibility trade-offs before recommending a marketing action.
