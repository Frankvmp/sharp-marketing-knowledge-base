# Standardisation and localisation

## Core statement

Global marketing requires choices between standardising the marketing mix across markets and adapting it to local conditions. Standardisation can reduce costs and support coordination, but insufficient adaptation can cause market failure.[^strategy]

## Definitions

Standardisation uses substantially similar products, positioning, communication, and operating approaches across markets. Localisation adapts one or more marketing-mix elements to market-specific conditions. Regionalisation recognises that similarities may be stronger within regions than across the whole world.[^strategy]

## Mechanism and relationships

Standardisation can create scale efficiencies in production, brand management, and communication. Localisation responds to differences in buyer preferences, laws, infrastructure, resources, costs, and competition. The appropriate choice can vary by element of the marketing mix and by market, so a mixed approach is often required.[^strategy]

## Evidence and data

The chapter describes global brands that use broadly consistent offers and communication, while also noting product, regulatory, infrastructure, and competitive constraints that make complete global uniformity impractical.[^strategy]

## Conditions and limits

Similarity among affluent or internationally connected buyer groups does not establish similarity for all buyers or categories. A strategy that works in one market may be unsuitable where usage conditions, regulations, or cultural meanings differ.[^strategy]

## Decision implications

An agent should identify the specific element being standardised or adapted, the evidence for cross-market similarity, and the cost or performance consequence of each choice. It should avoid treating "global" as a reason for uniformity by itself.
