# Heuristics and non-conscious choice

## Core statement

Much buyer thinking and behaviour is non-conscious. Buyers use heuristics—experience-based shortcuts, rules of thumb, hunches, and simple cues—to make workable decisions without exhaustive evaluation.[^heuristics]

## Mechanism

Conscious reasoning is limited and slow. Heuristics direct attention and simplify choice, for example by choosing a recognised brand, a market leader, the cheapest option, the most expensive option, or a middle-priced option. Such shortcuts can be biased, but can also save effort and perform adequately or better than more complex procedures in suitable situations.[^heuristics]

## Evidence and limits

Asking a buyer why a brand was purchased can elicit a rationalisation rather than a reliable account, because the buyer may not know the underlying process. Heuristics can create predictable biases, including social proof, anchoring, availability, familiarity, and processing fluency. They should not be treated as proof that buyers are irrational in every decision.[^nonconscious][^heuristics]

## Decision use

1. Identify the time, information, and attention available in the choice situation.
2. Identify cues likely to be used as shortcuts.
3. Distinguish a cue that helps a buyer decide from one that may mislead.
4. Treat self-reported reasons as evidence requiring validation, not as direct access to decision mechanisms.
5. Assess ethical implications when framing exploits a predictable shortcut.
