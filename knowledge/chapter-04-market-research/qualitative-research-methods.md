# Qualitative research methods

## Core statement

Qualitative research is suited to identifying what issues, behaviours, and meanings exist, explaining unexpected findings, and informing later measurement. It does not reliably estimate how common those issues are across a population.[^qualitative]

## Definitions

- **Qualitative data:** descriptive accounts of thoughts, feelings, language, and behaviour.
- **Focus group:** a group discussion, usually with eight to ten participants.
- **Depth interview:** an extended one-to-one interview, often useful for experts, complex decisions, or in-situ observation.
- **Observation:** recording actual behaviour as it occurs rather than relying on recall.[^qualitative][^methods]

## Method selection

Use focus groups when product interaction or group discussion can reveal issues, including an early check for widely negative reactions. Use depth interviews where individual experience needs time and context. Use observation where habitual, low-involvement, or repetitive behaviour is difficult to recall accurately, and combine it with exit interviews or other methods when explanations of observed behaviour are needed.[^methods]

## Evidence and data

Qualitative samples are commonly small because recruitment and interviewing are costly. They may generalise the existence of an issue, but not its prevalence. Focus groups can overrepresent highly involved people, allow moderator or dominant-participant influence, and give each person limited speaking time. Observation can document navigation, attention, browsing, and purchases, then be used qualitatively to discover behaviours or quantitatively to measure timings and proportions.[^qualitative][^methods]

## Conditions and limits

Focus groups are an unnatural setting for testing advertising that is normally consumed quickly and alone. Depth interviews can rely on weak memory and on difficult hypothetical questions. Qualitative interpretation is especially exposed to researcher influence, so clients should inspect prompts, respondent profiles, and the researchers' relevant experience.[^methods][^quality]

## Decision implications

Use qualitative evidence to discover and clarify the possible causes, language, and behaviours that a decision must account for. Do not convert a small qualitative sample into a population percentage. When prevalence matters, design a subsequent quantitative measurement.
