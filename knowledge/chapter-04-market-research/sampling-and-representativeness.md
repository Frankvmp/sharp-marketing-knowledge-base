# Sampling and representativeness

## Core statement

A sample can support generalisation only when it represents the population of interest and is selected in a way that limits bias. Sample size cannot repair systematic non-representativeness.[^sampling][^representativeness]

## Definitions

- **Research population:** the people or organisations meeting the criteria for the research; the population to which results are generalised.
- **Census:** research that includes every member of the population.
- **Probability sampling:** random selection from a population or database.
- **Non-probability sampling:** researcher selection or respondent self-selection.
- **Confidence interval:** the numerical range of likely sampling error around a result.[^sampling][^probability]

## Sampling procedure

1. Define the research population, including current and potential customers where relevant.
2. Identify the available sampling frame and contact route.
3. Decide whether a census is feasible; it is more feasible for small, diverse populations where sampling errors are costly.
4. Prefer random selection when results must be projected to the population.
5. Check demographic and response representativeness, response rate, non-response rate, and any exclusions.
6. Report uncertainty alongside sample results.[^sampling][^representativeness]

## Evidence and data

Probability methods include simple random, systematic, stratified, and clustered sampling. Convenience, judgement, quota, snowball, and advertised recruitment can help reach particular people or explore issues, but lack the statistical basis for confidence intervals and population projection. Customer lists often overrepresent heavier customers and omit light buyers, so they may not represent the full customer base.[^methods][^customer-samples]

## Conditions and limits

Random selection is not sufficient if response is low or differs systematically by group. Weighting can make a sample appear demographically closer to the population, but cannot make the few respondents in an underrepresented group typical of that group. Greater sample size reduces random sampling error with diminishing returns; it does not solve selection or response bias.[^representativeness][^sample-size]

## Decision implications

Do not use a result as a population estimate until the parent population, selection method, achieved response, and major exclusions are known. Use non-probability samples for discovery or access problems only with an explicit limit on generalisation.
