# Secondary data and contextual knowledge

## Core statement

Existing internal and external data should be reviewed before new collection. It can shape questions and measures, provide comparison points, or partially answer the problem, while established buyer-behaviour patterns provide a framework for interpreting and presenting the data.[^secondary]

## Definitions

- **Secondary data:** relevant data collected for a purpose other than the current project.
- **Internal secondary data:** prior research, sales figures, product history, frontline feedback, complaints, and similar organisational records.
- **External secondary data:** government statistics, academic and industry publications, syndicated reports, and databases.
- **Contextual knowledge:** established patterns that help explain results and reveal meaningful structures in data.[^secondary][^context]

## Reasoning procedure

1. Search the organisation's existing records and prior studies.
2. Identify external sources that overlap with the current information need.
3. Assess the gap between each source's original purpose and the present objective.
4. Use relevant findings to sharpen questions, approaches, measures, and comparisons.
5. Apply established buyer-behaviour knowledge when inspecting and ordering results.[^secondary][^context]

## Evidence and data

Secondary data rarely matches a new objective exactly. Complaint, return, refund, and review data may inform a satisfaction question without directly measuring satisfaction. Ordering brand data by market share instead of alphabetically can reveal differences in penetration and buying frequency that an alphabetical table obscures.[^secondary][^context]

## Conditions and limits

Existing data can reduce unnecessary collection but cannot substitute for evidence that directly answers an unresolved question. Continuously collected digital behavioural data can be large and immediate, but its volume does not remove the need to determine whether it fits the decision problem.[^secondary][^context]

## Decision implications

Start every research project with an evidence inventory. Use prior knowledge to form expectations and inspect patterns, but distinguish a useful benchmark from a direct answer to the new decision.
