# Mental availability in choice situations

## Core statement

Buying depends heavily on memory. A brand that is not recalled or noticed in the relevant choice situation has no opportunity to be purchased, regardless of product quality. Mental availability is the probability that a brand is easy to notice, recognise, or recall under particular circumstances.[^memory-choice]

## Definitions

- **Choice situation** is the time and place where a purchase decision is made.
- **Mental availability** is the ease with which a brand is noticed, recognised, or recalled in relevant situations.
- **Memory associations** are small pieces of information linked to a brand, such as category cues, colours, characters, occasions, or usage contexts.
- **Share of mind** is the quantity and quality of a brand's memory links.[^memory-choice]

## Mechanism and relationships

Some choice situations require recall before the buyer can search, travel to a store, or enter a query. Other situations present brands visually, making recognition and noticing important. Both rely on memory.

Memory is probabilistic rather than absolute: the same person may recall or notice a brand in one circumstance but not another. Wider and fresher associations increase the probability of retrieval across more buying situations. Advertising, buying and using the brand, word of mouth, and observation can build or refresh those associations.[^memory-choice]

Physical and mental availability reinforce each other. A brand with broader physical availability can convert more recalled occasions into purchase, while memorable advertising and distinctive packaging can make locations and products easier to notice.[^memory-choice]

## Evidence and data requirements

Measure memory against relevant category-entry cues and buying situations, rather than treating awareness as a fixed individual attribute. Record the cues that do and do not bring the brand to mind. Evaluate whether communication refreshes the desired associations, including visual and verbal links, in contexts relevant to purchase.[^memory-building]

## Conditions and limits

Memory erodes and competes with other information and competitor advertising. A single high awareness score does not guarantee retrieval in every buying situation. The appropriate objective is improving the probability of retrieval across relevant situations, not assuming universal recall.[^memory-choice]

## Decision implications

1. Identify the buying situations in which the brand needs to be recalled or noticed.
2. Identify the category and brand cues currently linked to those situations.
3. Use distinctive, easy-to-process verbal and visual cues to build or refresh associations.
4. Maintain activity over time so that competitor activity does not displace accessible memories.
5. Pair mental-availability work with physical availability where purchase must be easy to complete.
