# Planning limits and design thinking

## Core statement

Classic rational planning is useful but assumes relatively stable, predictable conditions. Design thinking complements it by reframing problems, engaging stakeholders, and exploring future possibilities rather than only optimising current market information.[^limits][^design]

## Definitions

Rational planning defines objectives and derives actions through ordered analysis. Design thinking is a problem-solving approach that uses empathy, stakeholder interaction, reframing, and future orientation to develop possible solutions.[^limits][^design]

## Mechanism and relationships

Structured analysis disciplines choices about markets, objectives, and resource allocation. Design thinking broadens the process by examining how a problem is framed, involving people affected by it, and imagining alternatives that may not follow from existing data alone.[^design]

## Evidence and data

The chapter contrasts the assumptions of conventional planning with a design-thinking orientation. It treats the two approaches as complementary, not as mutually exclusive replacements.[^limits][^design]

## Conditions and limits

Planning can overvalue existing organisational capability and underweight attractive opportunities that require stretching or developing capability. Reframing is especially relevant when conditions are uncertain or a familiar problem definition blocks new options.[^limits][^design]

## Decision implications

An agent should use structured analysis for discipline and evidence, while testing whether the current problem definition and capability assumptions remain valid. It should distinguish a justified capability stretch from an unsupported leap.
