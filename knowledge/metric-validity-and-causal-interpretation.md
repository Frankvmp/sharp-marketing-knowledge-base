# Metric validity and causal interpretation

## Core statement

A metric cannot be interpreted from its label, face-valid wording, or correlation with a business outcome. Its meaning must be established through its relationships with other measures and its response to real market changes. Causal claims require more than temporal proximity or association.[^metric-validity]

## Metric-validation procedure

1. Define the decision the metric is intended to inform.
2. Test how the measure varies with known related and unrelated variables.
3. Test how it responds when the relevant market condition changes.
4. Establish an expected benchmark or recurring pattern.
5. Investigate a deviation as either a measurement, analysis, interpretation, or market change.
6. Use the validated meaning, not the metric's name, when deciding what action follows.[^metric-validity]

## Benchmark: brand size effects

Larger brands usually score higher on memory and attitudinal measures because they have more users, who know more and report more about them. Comparisons must therefore account for brand penetration and usage. A small brand's lower perception score is not automatically evidence of inferior customer experience or weaker marketing.[^brand-size]

The **double jeopardy** pattern extends this benchmark: smaller brands generally have fewer buyers and those buyers buy less often. Competing brands share buyers, but larger brands are more likely to be known, considered, and easily purchased. The pattern establishes an expectation; an apparent deviation should be checked before it is treated as a strategic exception.[^double-jeopardy]

## Causal-reasoning procedure

1. Check temporal sequence: a proposed cause must occur before or with the outcome.
2. Treat temporal sequence as necessary but not sufficient.
3. Check whether the association could be coincidence, reverse causality, or a shared third cause.
4. Distinguish a necessary condition from a sufficient condition.
5. Prefer experiments, replications, and comparable exposure-versus-non-exposure evidence when estimating effects.
6. State the remaining uncertainty rather than converting a correlation into a precise causal return estimate.[^causality]

## Evidence and limits

Correlation is an association, not proof of causation. A marketing action and sales outcome can move together because sales influence spending decisions, because both respond to seasonal or economic conditions, or because they share a historical trend. High correlation can also describe a stable past relationship without predicting a future change.[^causality]

Historical models fitted to a single noisy dataset can produce multiple plausible but contradictory estimates. A model that does not make reliable predictions in similar future conditions has not established a dependable causal effect. Field experiments and individual-level exposure-and-purchase data can provide stronger evidence, although no method removes all uncertainty.[^causal-estimates]

## Decision implications

Use a measurement system to detect changes and generate hypotheses. Then test the hypothesis before allocating major resources on the assumption that one activity caused the result. Reject opaque proprietary measures unless their calculation, benchmark, and ability to anticipate meaningful change can be independently evaluated.
